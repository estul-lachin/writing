INC-002: A second botnet evicted the first and took over Honeypot #2

Honeypot #2 · Incident date 2026-11-16 (UTC) · Analyst: Estul Lachin

Final report · 48-hour observation closed 2026-11-18 08:00 UTC

Summary

At 08:03 UTC on 2026-11-16, a second botnet guessed the bait account's new weak password on Honeypot #2. Within three minutes it killed the miner left by the first intruder (INC-001); a minute later it deleted that group's SSH keys and planted its own. Over the next three hours it installed a disguised trojan, an XMRig miner and at least four kinds of persistence.

Its scanner then tried SSH on 509 internal addresses, including our gateway. Every packet was dropped on the victim, and the scanner later ran the 1 GB server out of memory and was killed by the kernel. The attacker never got root (sudo and su were refused) and no traffic got out.

This time detection was automatic: a rule added after INC-001 raised a High incident in Microsoft Sentinel. The investigation also found a blind spot, which a new rule closed the same day.

Detection

Rule 100210, added after INC-001, fires on any password login to the bait account. It matched at 08:03:19 and Sentinel raised High incident #603 automatically. Two minutes later, rule 100211 raised Medium incident #604 for the cron change. Nobody was watching at the time; the incidents were waiting at the next routine check.

What the attacker did

Compared with the careless script in INC-001, this group was organised: clear out the competition first, then hold the machine with several layers of persistence and disguise, and bring a scanner to spread further.

Impact assessment

No root, no traffic out, no contact with the gateway. The impact stayed inside the admin account's permissions and the server's memory and CPU.

Privilege escalation: none. The evidence comes from auditd and the journal, which are shipped off the victim:

  • sudo refused to run /var/tmp/fLSJqZDe as root ("user NOT in sudoers"), and su to root failed.
  • In the attacker's sessions, only SUID programs (sudo, su, passwd) and the login banner scripts that sshd runs ran with euid 0. Every attacker program ran as uid 1001. A check at the end of the observation found the same.

Egress: none. Between 08:00 and about 16:40 on 2026-11-16, the victim's tripwire blocked 22,417 outbound connections to the destinations below. Not one succeeded.

Lateral movement: attempted, all blocked. From 08:06 until the kernel killed it at 11:00:13, the scanner tried SSH on 509 internal addresses (the last blocked attempt reached us at 11:05:33 because monitoring was running about five minutes behind), including 132 attempts at the gateway's two addresses. The victim's own firewall dropped every packet, and without root the attacker could not turn that firewall off. The cloud firewall is a second layer behind it.

Resources: memory at the limit. The victim has 969 MB and no swap. The trojan holds 220 MB and the scanner peaked at about 160 MB, so at 11:00:13 the kernel's out-of-memory killer stopped the scanner. Our monitoring agent and admin access are set to be killed last, and kept running. The miner used under 1.5% CPU, most likely because it could not reach any pool.

Response and evidence handling

As in INC-001, the plan was to observe, not clean. One predefined isolation trigger was hit along the way. We weighed it and chose not to isolate, and record that decision openly here.

  1. Safety first. Confirmed the agent was online, rule 100200 had not fired and no attacker program ran as root before digging in.
  2. Read-only investigation over the WireGuard management channel (ps, ls, journalctl, crontab -l, ssh-keygen -lf). The attacker's command history came from Wazuh alerts on the gateway.
  3. Evidence. Archived /var/tmp, /home/admin, the crontab, and copies of the three running programs taken from /proc/<pid>/exe. The mysqld file had already been deleted and was recovered from memory. Nothing was extracted or run; hashes were computed by streaming files out of the archive.
  4. Closed the detection blind spot the same day (rule 100202).
  5. Observed for 48 hours, until 2026-11-18 08:00 UTC: no return visits, no third group, no root.

The isolation decision. Our rule was to isolate if the victim probed the gateway. The scanner did aim at the gateway's addresses, but every packet died on the victim. All three layers held: no root, the victim's firewall, and the cloud firewall. We did not isolate. We narrowed the trigger to "a probe actually reaches the gateway (rule 100200) or any sign of root", and added rule 100202 so that this kind of attempt raises an alert from now on.

Detection improvements

Why rule 100200 stayed silent: it only matches the gateway's own firewall log, so it fires only when a probe actually reaches the gateway. These probes were dropped earlier, on the victim, and were filed as routine blocked traffic. The earlier a packet is stopped, the less this rule saw.

Rule 100202 has not fired since: the scanner never came back after the kernel killed it. Its end-to-end path into Sentinel is therefore still to be confirmed. The rules added after INC-001 all proved themselves: 100210 raised the High incident, and 100211 and 100212 recorded every persistence step.

Lessons learned

The programs that did the real work were almost invisible to antivirus (4 and 1 detections). Behaviour monitoring caught them: auditd recorded every program run, file integrity monitoring recorded every persistence file, and the egress tripwire recorded every attempt to get out.

What worked

  • Rule 100210 from INC-001 raised the High incident on its own. No manual hunt this time.
  • A bait account without sudo rights stopped privilege escalation. The attacker came ready with a tool for it and never got to use it.
  • Egress control stopped everything again: pools, C2, and lateral movement. Without it, this honeypot would have become a launch pad against others.
  • Out-of-memory protection worked: the kernel killed the scanner, not our agent or management SSH.
  • Programs can be preserved from memory: the deleted mysqld was recovered from /proc/<pid>/exe.

What to improve

  • Design detection around what the attacker is trying to do, not where the packet ended up. Rule 100200 saw less the earlier a probe was stopped.
  • Write isolation triggers precisely. "The victim probes the gateway" was ambiguous and had to be interpreted after the fact.
  • 1 GB of memory is too tight. The attacker's tools filled it, and monitoring fell about five minutes behind.
  • The gateway disk is 80% full. The evidence archive held nine copies of the same 41 MB program (the attacker's seven plus our two memory copies); de-duplicate by hash before archiving.

Next steps

  • Confirm rule 100202 end to end in Sentinel at the next internal scan.
  • Tag tripwire logs with the owning process, which would also explain why INC-001's pool was still being contacted.
  • Decide when phase 2 ends, then rebuild the victim from a clean image, with 2 GB of memory.
